**Credential Access** is the exploration page that answers one blunt question: *which process touched which class of secret?* It is built on `file_open` over the paths where macOS keeps its secrets — Keychains, SSH keys, browser cookies, cloud credentials, dotenv files, certificates — and it is the deeper companion to the [Credential & Privacy](/dashboard/endpoint/credential-privacy) detection page. Where that page raises findings, this page lets you *roam* the credential surface of a Mac: every reach for a secret, every process doing the reaching, mapped as a graph.

The whole page rides on the [foundation](/dashboard/endpoint/foundation): every row in `endpoint_events` already carries the [code-signing verdict](/macos-collector/event-data-model#the-code-signature-verdict) and the [owning application](/macos-collector/event-data-model#application-attribution-every-event-the-macos-differentiator). That is the killer move here. On Linux, "a process read `login.keychain-db`" is the whole story. On macOS, the kernel also tells you *what signed that process and whether to trust it* — so a `developer_id` browser reading its own cookies is routine, and an `adhoc` binary reading `login.keychain-db` is a credential heist in one row.

<Note>
Sensitive-path access is one of the categories ESensor [never filters](/macos-collector/event-coverage#what-is-never-filtered): opens of credential files, SSH keys, and tokens are preserved even when the same path-class would otherwise be noisy. This page sees the full credential surface, not a sampled one.
</Note>

## Page layout

The header is the standard Endpoint chrome: the selected Mac (`device_name`), the time range, and the agent status bar described in the [shared layout](/dashboard/endpoint/foundation#shared-layout). Two controls are specific to this page:

- **Signature filter** — narrow the whole page to one or more `code_signature` tiers (`platform` / `developer_id` / `adhoc` / `unsigned_or_invalid`). Set it to `adhoc, unsigned_or_invalid` and the bipartite graph collapses to *only the untrusted reaches* — the single most useful view on the page.
- **Investigate events** — jumps to Investigate scoped to credential `file_open` activity for the current host.

### KPI strip

Four counters frame the credential surface for the selected window:

| KPI | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Credential opens** | Total `file_open` events landing on a recognized secret path |
| **Secret files touched** | Distinct `filename` paths across all classes |
| **Processes reaching for secrets** | Distinct responsible apps / `comm` that opened any secret |
| **Secret classes** | How many of the eight classes saw any activity in range |

On a representative host the strip reads roughly **242k credential opens** across the classes — the bulk of which is benign tooling reading its own config, which is exactly why the per-access trust verdict matters.

## Secret classes

Every credential open is classified into one of eight **secret classes** by its path. The classification is a `multiIf` over `filename` — the same expression the page runs and the [SQL below](#querying-endpoint_events) makes explicit.

| Class | Recognized by | Live volume | Reaching procs | Danger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `generic_secret` | `*credential*`, `*secret*`, `*token*`, `*.p12`, `*.pfx` | ~135k | 81 | — |
| `certificate` | `*.pem`, `*.crt`, `cert*` | ~57k | — | — |
| `keychain` | `Keychains/*`, `*.keychain`, `*keychain-db` | ~25k | 80 | **high** |
| `browser_cookies` | `*/Cookies` | ~18k | 12 | — |
| `cloud_cred` | `~/.aws`, `~/.config/gcloud`, `~/.kube`, `~/.docker/config.json` | ~7k | — | **high** |
| `ssh_key` | `~/.ssh`, `id_rsa`, `id_ed25519` | — | — | **high** |
| `dotenv` | `*.env` | — | — | — |

<Warning>
**`keychain`, `ssh_key`, and `cloud_cred` are danger-weighted.** They unlock the most — the system credential store, remote-host access, and your entire cloud estate — so edges into them are rendered with heavier weight and warmer color, and they sort to the top of every panel. A single untrusted reach into one of these classes outranks ten thousand routine certificate reads.
</Warning>

## The bipartite graph

The centerpiece is a **bipartite graph**. Processes sit on the left; secret classes sit on the right. An edge connects a process to every class it opened, and the edge weight is the **open count** — so the thick ropes are the heavy, routine readers and the thin threads are the rare reaches worth a second look.

- **Process nodes** (left) are the responsible apps, folded back from helpers via `application_id` — so `Google Chrome Helper` collapses into **Google Chrome**, the way [attribution](/macos-collector/event-data-model#application-attribution-every-event-the-macos-differentiator) intends. Each node is tinted by its worst `code_signature` tier: a green `developer_id` node and a red `unsigned_or_invalid` node read differently at a glance.
- **Class nodes** (right) are the eight secret classes, with the three danger-weighted classes pinned and accented.
- **Edges** carry the open count as weight, and inherit the danger color of the class they land on.

The point of the layout is that **theft has a shape**. A normal Mac is a few fat ropes — `security` into `keychain`, browsers into `browser_cookies`, Python into `certificate` — and almost nothing else. An attacker tool shows up as a *new* process node with thin edges fanning out to *several* danger-weighted classes at once: one binary reaching for the Keychain *and* `~/.ssh` *and* `~/.aws` is the fan-out signature of credential harvesting, and it is visually obvious the instant it appears.

### Drill-down

The graph is a launchpad, not a dead end. Clicking either side opens a detail panel from the right:

| Click | Panel shows |
| --- | --- |
| **A secret class** | The class's **top files** (by open count), the **processes reaching in** (each with its signature verdict and open count), and a class-scoped timeline |
| **A process** | The **classes it touched**, **sample paths** per class, its signing identity (`signing_id`, `team_id`, `code_signature`, `cdhash`), and an Investigate jump |

So you can enter from the *secret* — "who is touching the Keychain?" — or from the *process* — "what did this `adhoc` binary reach for?" — and the two views are the same data pivoted.

## Routine vs. alarming: pairing access with trust

This is the page's reason to exist, and it is worth stating plainly. The path alone is ambiguous — *everything* reads the Keychain. The `code_signature` verdict on the **same row** is what separates the daily grind from the breach.

| Access | Class | `code_signature` | Read as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `security` → `login.keychain-db` | `keychain` | `platform` | Routine — the OS credential tool doing its job |
| `git-credential-osxkeychain` → `login.keychain-db` | `keychain` | `developer_id` | Routine — git fetching a stored token |
| `securityd` → `System.keychain` | `keychain` | `platform` | Routine — system daemon, system store |
| `Google Chrome Helper` → `Cookies-journal` | `browser_cookies` | `developer_id` | Routine — Chrome reading its own cookies |
| `python3` → `certifi/cacert.pem` | `certificate` | `platform` | Routine — Python's CA bundle |
| *unknown binary* → `login.keychain-db` | `keychain` | `adhoc` | **Alarming** — untrusted code in the credential store |
| *dropped payload* → `~/.aws/credentials` | `cloud_cred` | `unsigned_or_invalid` | **Alarming** — your cloud keys, read by nothing you trust |

The first five rows are the fat ropes you expect; the last two are the thin threads you hunt for. Same paths, same event type — only the trust tier differs. That is why the **Signature filter** is the fastest way to use this page: flip it to `adhoc, unsigned_or_invalid` and everything routine disappears.

## Querying `endpoint_events`

The graph and panels are all views over one query: classify the `filename`, then group by owning app and class, with the trust verdict carried through so you can filter on it. This is the page's centerpiece query — the same `multiIf` it uses to build the secret classes.

```sql
-- Credential reaches per app and secret class, with the trust verdict.
-- Flip the WHERE on code_signature to isolate untrusted reaches.
SELECT
    application_display_name AS app,
    code_signature,
    multiIf(
        filename LIKE '%Keychains/%'
          OR filename LIKE '%.keychain%'
          OR filename LIKE '%keychain-db%',          'keychain',
        filename LIKE '%/.ssh/%'
          OR filename LIKE '%id_rsa%'
          OR filename LIKE '%id_ed25519%',           'ssh_key',
        filename LIKE '%/Cookies%',                   'browser_cookies',
        filename LIKE '%/.aws/%'
          OR filename LIKE '%/.config/gcloud/%'
          OR filename LIKE '%/.kube/%'
          OR filename LIKE '%/.docker/config.json%',  'cloud_cred',
        filename LIKE '%.env%',                        'dotenv',
        filename LIKE '%.pem%'
          OR filename LIKE '%.crt%'
          OR filename LIKE '%cert%',                   'certificate',
        filename LIKE '%credential%'
          OR filename LIKE '%secret%'
          OR filename LIKE '%token%'
          OR filename LIKE '%.p12%'
          OR filename LIKE '%.pfx%',                   'generic_secret',
        'other'
    ) AS secret_class,
    count()        AS opens,
    min(event_time) AS first_seen,
    max(event_time) AS last_seen
FROM endpoint_events
WHERE event_type = 'file_open'
  AND device_name = 'Zin''s MacBook Pro'
  AND secret_class != 'other'
  -- the line that turns this into a threat hunt:
  AND code_signature IN ('adhoc', 'unsigned_or_invalid')
GROUP BY app, code_signature, secret_class
ORDER BY opens DESC;
```

Drop the last `AND` and the result is the whole credential surface — the data behind the KPI strip and the fat ropes. Keep it, and you get only the reaches no signed vendor stands behind. On the [Linux collector this query cannot be written](/macos-collector/event-data-model#the-code-signature-verdict): there is no per-event signing verdict to filter on.

<Note>
Like every Endpoint page, this is **metadata, not content**. Credential Access shows *that* a process opened `login.keychain-db`, signed by whom, on behalf of which app — never the secret inside. See [what the collector does not capture](/macos-collector/event-data-model#what-the-collector-does-not-capture).
</Note>
