The **Credential & Privacy** page answers one question: *who reached for the secrets and the sensors on this Mac, and should they have?* It watches the four places an attacker goes for credentials and the two pieces of hardware they go to for surveillance — the **Keychain**, **browser-saved passwords and cookies**, the **TCC privacy database**, and the **camera and microphone** — and surfaces the accesses that do not fit.

It is one of the five **detection** pages built on the shared layout described in [the Endpoint foundation](/dashboard/endpoint/foundation#shared-layout). Like its siblings, it resolves to a single Mac: pick an endpoint, and the page becomes a severity-ranked list of findings about credential and privacy access on that host. Because every underlying row carries [code-signing trust and application attribution](/macos-collector/event-data-model#code-signing-identity-every-event-the-macos-differentiator), a finding here is never just "the Keychain was read" — it is "**which** app, signed by **whom**, read it, and whether that app is the one that owns it."

<Note>
This is the curated, ranked **detection** view. For the unfiltered version — *every* credential-file open rendered as a process↔secret graph, with no severity model in the way — use the [Credential Access](/dashboard/endpoint/credential-access) exploration page. This page tells you what to worry about; that page lets you trace everything.
</Note>

## Page layout

The page uses the [shared detection layout](/dashboard/endpoint/foundation#shared-layout). Top to bottom:

| Region | What it shows here |
| --- | --- |
| **Agent header** | The selected Mac: device name, risk level, last-seen, ES / Network Extension run state, policy version, and live credential/privacy finding counts. |
| **Summary cards** | One card per activity group — **Keychain · Browser credentials · TCC database · Camera / Mic** — each with its finding count and worst severity. |
| **Timeline** | A 60-minute, per-minute chart of credential/privacy findings. A camera light that flicks on at 02:14 is a spike you can click straight into. |
| **Severity / Status filters** | Narrow to a [severity](/dashboard/endpoint/foundation#findings-and-severity) (`critical` → `info`) or to how the agent responded (`blocked`, `alerted`, `logged`). |
| **Findings table** | Grouped, collapsible: **Time · Type / Target · App · Severity · Status**. Repeat access by the same app to the same store folds into one `N×` row. |
| **Finding detail** | The six-part breakdown — **What happened · Process responsible · What was touched · Why it matters · Evidence · Recommended action** — with the trust verdict and owning app in *Process responsible*. |

## Activity groups

Each summary card is one of four activity groups. Every group is a different sensitive target; the detection logic across all of them is the same — *compare the app reaching for the target against the app that is supposed to own it, and weight by the [trust verdict](/macos-collector/event-data-model#the-code_signature-verdict).*

| Group | Target | Built on | Primary MITRE technique |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Keychain** | `login.keychain-db`, `System.keychain` | `file_open` on the keychain paths | [T1555.001 — Keychain](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1555/001/) |
| **Browser credentials** | Saved-password and cookie stores (Chrome `Login Data` / `Cookies`, Safari, Firefox) | `file_open` on the browser profile databases | [T1539 — Steal Web Session Cookie](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1539/) |
| **TCC database** | `TCC.db` (user and system) | `file_open` on the TCC store | [T1555 — Credentials from Password Stores](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1555/) |
| **Camera / Mic** | Hardware capture devices | Responsible-app attribution on capture-helper activity | [T1123 — Audio Capture](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1123/), [T1125 — Video Capture](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1125/) |

### Keychain

The macOS Keychain is the operating system's own credential vault — Wi-Fi passwords, certificates, app secrets, and saved logins all live in `login.keychain-db` and `System.keychain`. A legitimate keychain access comes from `securityd`, a `platform`-signed Apple binary, or from the app that stored the item. A finding fires when **anything else** opens those files: a `developer_id` app reaching past its own keychain items, or — the high-severity case — an `adhoc` or `unsigned_or_invalid` binary reading the vault at all.

Because the [trust verdict rides on the `file_open` row itself](/macos-collector/event-data-model#code-signing-identity-every-event-the-macos-differentiator), "a non-owner process read the keychain" is a property of the event, not a correlation you build after the fact.

### Browser credentials

Browsers keep saved passwords and session cookies in per-profile SQLite databases — Chrome's `Login Data` and `Cookies`, the equivalents in Safari and Firefox. The legitimate reader of `com.google.Chrome`'s `Login Data` is `com.google.Chrome`. A finding fires when a **different** application opens that file — the textbook cookie-and-password infostealer pattern (T1539), where a dropped binary copies the cookie store to lift live sessions without ever touching a password.

The `application_id` of the **owning** browser and the `application_id` of the **process doing the read** are both on the row, so the mismatch that defines this technique is a single comparison.

### TCC privacy database

`TCC.db` is the database behind macOS privacy: it records which apps you have granted camera, microphone, screen-recording, Full Disk Access, and Accessibility. Reading or writing it directly — rather than going through the OS prompt — is how malware enumerates what it is already allowed to do, or quietly grants itself a permission. A finding fires on any non-platform access to the user or system `TCC.db`.

<Warning>
The dashboard detects TCC access by observing **`file_open` on the `TCC.db` path** and attributing it to the responsible app. It does not yet ingest the OS-level permission-grant event itself — `NOTIFY_TCC_MODIFY` is a [roadmap hook, not currently emitted](/macos-collector/event-coverage#roadmap-hooks-not-yet-enabled). What you see today is *who touched the privacy database*, with full trust and app attribution; the structured grant/deny stream lands when that ES event is enabled.
</Warning>

### Camera / Mic

This group answers a question users ask constantly and rarely get a straight answer to: *the camera light came on — what turned it on?* Each finding names the **responsible application** and the **time** the capture device was active, derived from the [responsible-app attribution](/macos-collector/event-data-model#application-attribution-every-event-the-macos-differentiator) on the capture-helper processes that mediate hardware access.

In live data on a quiet host, the camera activations attribute to **`ContinuityCaptureAgent`** (the Apple `platform`-signed agent behind Continuity Camera) and the microphone activations attribute to **`Discord Helper (Renderer)`** — a `developer_id` app folded back, via `application_id`, to **Discord**. That folding is the point: the raw actor is a transient renderer helper that means nothing on its own; the finding reads `Discord`, because [helper processes fold to their owning app](/macos-collector/event-data-model#application-attribution-every-event-the-macos-differentiator).

The `code_signature` verdict is what separates a benign activation from an alarming one. A `developer_id` conferencing app using the mic is expected — `info`. An `adhoc` or `unsigned_or_invalid` binary activating the camera or microphone is the surveillance case, and it ranks `high` to `critical`.

| Responsible app | Device | `code_signature` | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `ContinuityCaptureAgent` | Camera | `platform` | `info` |
| `Discord` (via `Discord Helper (Renderer)`) | Microphone | `developer_id` | `info` |
| an `adhoc` / unsigned binary | Camera or Mic | `adhoc` / `unsigned_or_invalid` | `high` → `critical` |

## Reading a finding

Selecting any row opens the standard six-part [finding detail](/dashboard/endpoint/foundation#shared-layout). On this page the load-bearing panels are:

| Panel | What it carries here |
| --- | --- |
| **What happened** | The action — e.g. "process opened `login.keychain-db` for read" — and the timestamp. |
| **Process responsible** | The acting `comm`, its `signing_id` / `team_id` / `cdhash`, the `code_signature` verdict, and the owning `application_display_name`. This is where "is this the app that *should* be doing this" is answered. |
| **What was touched** | The target store or capture device, and for file-backed groups the exact `filename`. |
| **Why it matters** | The mapped MITRE technique and the trust-based rationale. |
| **Evidence** | The raw `endpoint_events` row(s) behind the finding, expandable from the `N×` group. |
| **Recommended action** | The triage step — quarantine the binary, revoke a TCC grant, rotate the exposed credential. |

## Querying the underlying data

Every finding on this page is a curated view over `endpoint_events`. The same rows are queryable directly. This is the query the **Keychain** group is built on — every non-platform binary that opened the keychain, with the trust verdict that drives its severity:

```sql
-- Who read the Keychain — and is it a trusted app or not?
SELECT event_time,
       device_name,
       application_display_name,         -- owning app (helpers folded in)
       comm,                             -- the acting binary
       signing_id,
       team_id,
       code_signature,                   -- platform / developer_id / adhoc / unsigned_or_invalid
       filename
FROM endpoint_events
WHERE event_type = 'file_open'
  AND filename IN ('login.keychain-db', 'System.keychain')
  AND is_platform_binary = 0             -- exclude securityd and other OS components
ORDER BY
  code_signature IN ('unsigned_or_invalid', 'adhoc') DESC,  -- untrusted first
  event_time DESC;
```

Swap the `filename` predicate for the browser credential databases to drive the **Browser credentials** group, or for the `TCC.db` paths to drive the **TCC database** group. The shape never changes — that is the whole advantage of [trust and identity living on every row](/macos-collector/event-data-model). On the Linux collector this query cannot be expressed: there is no per-event code-signing verdict to rank by.

## Detection vs. exploration

This page is deliberately opinionated. It shows a **finite, ranked list of findings** — the accesses a severity model flagged as worth your attention — and it stops there. That is the right tool when you want to know whether anything is wrong right now.

When you want to know *everything* — every credential-file open on the host, benign or not, laid out as a process↔secret graph you can trace in any direction — switch to the [**Credential Access**](/dashboard/endpoint/credential-access) page. It runs on the same `file_open` rows with no curation in the way: it is the exploration counterpart to this page's detection. A common workflow is to triage here, spot a suspicious keychain read, then pivot to Credential Access to see what *else* that process touched.

For coverage of how these techniques roll up across the fleet, see the [ATT&CK Matrix](/dashboard/endpoint/attack-matrix); to follow the responsible process back to its origin, see [Process Lineage](/dashboard/endpoint/process-lineage).

## What this page never sees

The [collector boundary](/macos-collector/event-data-model#what-the-collector-does-not-capture) holds here as everywhere in the Endpoint section: this is **metadata, not content**. The page reports *that* a process opened the keychain or the cookie store, signed by whom, on behalf of which app — never the passwords inside, the cookie values, or the contents of `TCC.db`. It does not capture camera frames, microphone audio, keystrokes, or screen content — only *that the device was activated*, and by which application. The secrets stay where they are; only the access to them is observed.
